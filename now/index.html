Now | November 2026

What has my
attention.

The first week of November on the coast has its own feel. Mornings are cooler, the bay looks different, and the light gets low and gold early in the afternoon. The tropics are done talking. What is left is the quieter side of the Gulf in fall, the birds coming in, the water settling, and a city getting ready to vote. This is the time of year I like best here, when the place slows down just enough to notice itself.

In progress.

Following the city election, which lands on November 3. Corpus Christi is picking a mayor and city council, with nine seats up, including three at large and Districts 1 through 5. The at-large race has the biggest field, fifteen named candidates, and four of the five district seats are open fields with no incumbent running. Two items on the ballot are worth reading closely. Proposition A is a Crime Control and Prevention District sales tax measure. Proposition B is a charter amendment on an industrial water drought surcharge, which ties straight back to the water questions this city cannot stop asking.

Local elections decide the things you actually feel, what you pay, what gets fixed, and who answers when the water is a problem. They also tend to get the smallest turnout. Whoever shows up gets a bigger say than they probably realize.

Paying attention to.

The whooping cranes are the other thing I watch this time of year. The last wild migrating flock winters around Aransas National Wildlife Refuge, just up the coast, and the Fish and Wildlife Service estimated about 540 birds there last winter, including at least 57 juveniles. That is slightly under the prior year, within the margin of error, and the count has held above 500 for more than nine years running. When the species was listed in 1967, roughly 50 were left. Eight cranes even spent last winter at a refuge in Oklahoma, the first time that has been recorded. Fall means they are on the way back.

It is a good reminder that a recovery can be slow and still be real. A bird that was down to a few dozen now crosses the whole middle of the continent to land in the same marshes every year. Most people here will never look up when they pass over. I try to.
