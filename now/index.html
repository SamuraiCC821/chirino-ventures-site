Now | October 2026

What has my
attention.

Hurricane season is finally loosening its grip. This year turned out to be the quietest in Atlantic records going back to 1950, held down by a strong El Nino that kept ripping storms apart before they could organize into anything real. Gulf water temperatures are starting to drop with the first real cold fronts moving through, and the daily habit of checking the tropics is fading into something closer to reflex than necessity. Nobody here says the season is over out loud. The old rule still holds, it only takes one storm, but attention in Corpus Christi is already shifting toward what fall actually brings, cooler mornings, lower humidity, and a return to noticing things that are not weather.

In progress.

Following the Corpus Christi LNG expansion as it moves through federal review. Cheniere's Stage Three came online in August and pushed the plant's capacity to roughly 25 million tons a year. The proposed Stage Four, four more production trains, a pipeline stretching over 25 miles, and a third marine berth, would put this facility on track to become the third largest LNG export terminal in the world by 2031. The port already moved more than 200 million tons through the ship channel last year. What is less visible from the highway is what that growth means for dredging schedules, channel traffic, and the quiet decisions about how deep and how wide this waterway keeps getting cut.

Also tracking the city's Minor Home Repair Program, which opens for applications this month. Thirty eight homeowners will receive grants up to $24,999 for repairs tied to health and safety. It is a small program next to the numbers coming out of the port, but it says something true about this city, a place that is simultaneously becoming one of the country's largest energy export hubs and still has longtime residents who need a grant program just to fix a roof or a foundation. Both things are real here at the same time, and neither one cancels out the other.

Paying attention to.

The desalination fight keeps circling without landing. Corpus Christi's city council voted down its own seawater desalination plant again in September, only months after talks had reopened, while a separate facility on Harbor Island finished the permitting process for 50 million gallons a day under a different operator entirely. The sticking point is still the one it has been for years, where the brine goes, since the leftover salt concentrate runs about twice as strong as seawater and someone has to decide whose stretch of the bay absorbs it. Watching this one closely, because whoever answers that question first sets the pattern everyone else follows.

Underneath all of it, the ordinary machinery of the city keeps turning. New assistant city manager positions were just filled, the Watergarden fountain downtown is closed for repairs after a vehicle hit it, and CCISD held its State of the District address this month. None of it makes headlines outside the city, and most of it will not matter in five years. But this is what a place actually sounds like day to day, underneath the bigger numbers about energy exports and water rights.
