Now | September 2026

What has my
attention.

We are in the middle of hurricane season now, the statistical peak landing somewhere in the next two weeks, and the Gulf knows it. Water temperatures are high and the tropics have been active since July. This is the part of the year when living on the coast is not a metaphor. You watch the National Hurricane Center the way other people check sports scores, casually at first, then with more intention when something organizes. The city is not panicking. It rarely does. But there is a low hum of preparedness that shows up in the grocery store and the gas stations, and it is honest.

In progress.

Working through the Nueces River Authority's recent drought contingency reports. South Texas water looks fine until it does not, and the upstream patterns have been stressed for a few years running now. Desalination keeps getting mentioned as the long answer for Corpus Christi but the conversation always stalls somewhere between capital cost and brine disposal. What is interesting is not the technology problem but the political economy around who makes the call and when. That is where most infrastructure decisions actually live.

Reading the Corps of Engineers updates on Packery Channel and the north Padre shoreline erosion data. The channel is supposed to help stabilize that stretch of beach but the numbers are mixed and the sand budget along the barrier island is a topic most people here understand only when their favorite fishing spot disappears. The math is slow but it compounds.

Paying attention to.

The Texas General Land Office and the beach access disputes that keep resurfacing along the coast. Texas has strong public beach access law on paper, but enforcement is inconsistent and private development keeps testing the edges. There have been quiet legal actions in multiple counties this year. The outcomes tend to set precedent in ways that travel down the coast, so what happens in Galveston eventually matters in Nueces County too.

The shrimping fleet out of Port Aransas and Aransas Pass. It has been shrinking for a long time and the reasons are layered: imported shrimp pricing, fuel costs, crew recruitment, NOAA bycatch regulations. What is left is older boats, older captains, and a generational handoff that is not really happening. The industry is not collapsing in one dramatic moment. It is just quietly getting smaller each season and fewer people are writing about it.

Mid-September coming. The water is warm and so is the season's attention.
